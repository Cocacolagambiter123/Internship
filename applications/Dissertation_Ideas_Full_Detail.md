### Terms used throughout

- **Event study**: measure how a price moves in a short window around a dated announcement, compared with ordinary days.
- **Surprise**: the released number minus what the market expected (a consensus forecast), or a price jump in a tight window around a release.
- **Instrument / IV**: using an exogenous event (something the market did not cause) to isolate one source of price variation.
- **HAC or Newey-West standard errors**: standard errors that allow for autocorrelation and changing variance, so t-statistics are honest.
- **Placebo**: run the same test on days or series where nothing should happen; if you find an "effect" there, your design is broken.
- **Mincer-Zarnowitz regression**: regress the outcome on the forecast; an unbiased forecast gives intercept 0 and slope 1.
- **Diebold-Mariano (DM) test**: a test of whether two forecasts differ in accuracy, with a small-sample correction (Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold, HLN).
- **CRPS / log score**: scores for a whole forecast distribution, not just its centre.
- **PIT and Christoffersen tests**: checks on whether a stated 95% band really contains the outcome 95% of the time, and whether misses cluster.
- **Difference-in-differences (DiD)**: compare the change in a treated series across a regime date with the change in an untreated control.
- **Kelly**: the bet-sizing rule that maximises long-run growth given an estimated edge and variance; a log-score gap between two forecasters converts into a Kelly growth rate.
- **Reality check (White 2000) and Romano-Wolf**: corrections for having tried several rules or tests, so one lucky result is not reported as a finding.
- **Variance risk premium (VRP)**: implied variance (what options charge) minus the variance that is then realised.
- **OVX**: Cboe's 30-day implied volatility index for crude oil, built from USO options.
- **TTF, NBP, Henry Hub**: the Dutch, British and US natural gas benchmark prices.
- **EIA, STEO, WPSR, WNGSR**: the US Energy Information Administration; its monthly Short-Term Energy Outlook; its Weekly Petroleum Status Report (Wednesday 10:30 ET); its Weekly Natural Gas Storage Report (Thursday 10:30 ET).
- **MOMR, OMR**: OPEC's Monthly Oil Market Report; the IEA's Oil Market Report.
- **SPR**: the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve.
- **Breakeven inflation**: nominal bond yield minus inflation-linked yield; the market's inflation expectation plus a risk premium.

### J. Who Hedges the Barrel?

**Scores:** originality 8.0, quant appeal 8.3, prize and mark 8.0, feasibility 7.7, Gifuni fit 7.0. Ranked first overall by all three judges.

**The question.** Using Känzig's OPEC-announcement-day oil supply surprises, does a petro-currency's same-day "oil beta" (how much it moves per unit of oil news) shrink, kink or turn one-sided when its government hedges the country's oil exposure for it? Three cases: Norway's pre-announced daily krone conversions for the oil fund (against unhedged Canada); Russia's 2017-2022 budget-rule purchases of foreign currency, which switched on only when oil was above a cut-off price; and Mexico's annual put-option hedge, which protects only the downside. And is whatever beta remains tradable after a reality check?

**One-sentence hook.** Norway ships its oil money abroad by rule, Russia bought dollars only when Urals was above the budget price, and Mexico buys puts; I test whether these sovereign hedges show up as smaller, kinked or one-sided oil betas in their currencies on the days OPEC speaks.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** Känzig (2021, AER, DOI 10.1257/aer.20190964) shows the dollar depreciates after adverse oil supply news, most against exporters' currencies, but estimates no individual petro-currency betas and no institutions. Rüth, Oruc, Van der Veken and Zhang (2026, "When OPEC Speaks", University of Erfurt working paper; only the news summary was found) show the dollar's reaction flipped sign in the shale era with panel local projections across importer and exporter currencies; the summary mentions no fiscal rules, fund flows or hedges, but you must read the paper in week 1. Ahmed (2020, Economics Letters 189, MPRA 98319) explains a 30-currency cross-section around the single 2019 Abqaiq attack with export intensity, reserves and current accounts. Habib, Bützer and Stracca (2016, IMF Economic Review 64, DOI 10.1057/imfer.2016.9) find, with monthly shocks, that exporters absorb oil shocks through reserves rather than exchange rates. The ruble's weaker oil link after the 2017 budget rule is documented descriptively (Fedoseeva 2018, International Economics 156; BOFIT Weekly 35/2018; Bank of Russia WP 64 and WP 107). Norges Bank Economic Commentaries 2/2014 and 1/2016 argue the conversion mechanism should not move the krone, without a test. An NHH thesis finds higher NOK variance on Norges Bank FX-purchase announcement days to 2013, with no return reaction and no later sample. Bjørnland and Thorsrud (2016, JAE, DOI 10.1002/jae.2669) cover Norway's fiscal rule but not FX. Baghestani, Chazi and Khallaf (2019, OPEC Energy Review, DOI 10.1111/opec.12234) and Ma and Valencia (IMF WP 18/35) cover oil-peso links and the put hedge's welfare value, not the FX reaction to the hedge. Degasperi (2023/2026; Warwick WERPS 1464 / Bank of Italy TD 1516) supplies a second, cleaner OPEC surprise series. Akram (2020), Olstad, Filis and Degiannakis (2021), Baek and Kim (2025) and Moshiri (2015) use structural or volatility designs for exporter currencies, none with OPEC-day surprises and institutional treatments. Mori and Peersman (2024, Marco Fanno WP 314) show financial variables Granger-cause the Känzig instrument, which is why VIX and S&P 500 controls are needed.

What no paper found has done: (1) estimate currency-by-currency OPEC-day betas with within-country time variation, and (2) explain them with the actual hedging institutions and their dated regime changes, including Norges Bank's monthly pre-announced amounts as a continuous treatment, Russia's rule with its cut-off price and the February 2017, March-December 2020 and January 2022 regime dates in a difference-in-differences against CAD and NOK, and Mexico's downside-only puts predicting asymmetric betas. The tradability test with a reality check and Kelly sizing is also new.

**Why Dr Gifuni will find it interesting, and what he can advise on from public work.** Topic 3 of his brief is "how alternative central institutions are responding to the future projections of commodity prices". Russia's cut-off price is a published oil-price rule, Mexico's strike is the budget's oil-price assumption, and Norway's conversions follow projected petroleum revenue, so this tests whether markets price institutions' responses to commodity projections. It also sits in his "country-specific effects of energy price shocks" and "spillovers" themes and uses his methods: Kilian and Baumeister-Kilian oil VARs (his model-uncertainty paper), the Känzig instrument he cites, high-frequency event studies (his Brexit paper), and forecast-evaluation discipline. From public knowledge he can advise on instrument choice and averaging across Känzig and Degasperi, event windows and HAC inference, the 2014 and 2020 oil collapses, and an economic-value evaluation he has noted is absent from his own work. He is an oil person, so he will find it interesting. Be honest in the proposal: there is no text component, and FX is not his field. Frame the topic-3 fit explicitly (fiscal rules and hedging programmes as institutional responses to commodity projections) rather than claiming a verbatim match.

**Why a quant interviewer will like it, and what it proves.** This is how an FX macro desk thinks: the oil beta of NOK versus CAD versus MXN, why the krone "should" move and does not, and whether a sovereign's hedge or fund flow is already priced. You turn institutional detail into testable payoff shapes (a kink at the budget price, a one-sided beta from puts, a sign-dependent beta from pre-announced flows), handle the dollar common factor properly, use an identified high-frequency instrument with its known critiques, run a difference-in-differences across regime dates with a control currency, and then ask whether the residual beta is tradable after costs, a reality check and Kelly sizing. Skills proved: event-study and panel econometrics, instrument validity checks, asymmetric and threshold regressions, bootstrap and multiple-testing control, risk-adjusted strategy evaluation, and poker-style thinking about who is hedged and who is exposed.

1. Weeks 1-5, baseline betas (a complete table on its own). Reproduce Känzig's exporters-versus-importers result and Rüth et al.'s time variation as checks. Deliverable: to my knowledge the first currency-by-currency table of high-frequency oil-news betas.
2. Weeks 5-7, the cross-section. Pool a currency-day panel with net oil exports/GDP interacted with the surprise (currency fixed effects, date-clustered errors), add the Degasperi supply-versus-information split, and identify residual under-reactors (prediction: NOK, RUB after 2017, MXN on the downside). Deliverable: do exposure fundamentals explain betas, and who reacts less than exposure implies.
3. Weeks 7-10, Norway versus Canada (core mechanism). Two tests. (a) Interact NOK's OPEC-day surprise with the sign and size of that month's pre-announced conversion flow, with CAD as the control currency in the same regression. (b) An event study of the 10:00 last-business-day announcement itself: regress EUR/NOK's day-0 return on the surprise in the announced amount relative to a stated expectation (previous month, or an AR forecast), 2008-2026. Deliverable: whether the fiscal mechanism, not just oil, explains the krone puzzle; stands alone.
4. Weeks 10-13, Russia. Difference-in-differences of RUB's OPEC-day beta across regimes (pre-February 2017 float; February 2017-January 2022 rule purchases; March-December 2020 sales window) with CAD and NOK controls, pre-trend checks, sanction-date dummies (March 2014, April 2018), post-February 2022 excluded. State honestly that the cut-off is crossed only in 2020, so the "kink" is a regime test confounded with COVID; report it as pre-specified. Deliverable: a state-dependent beta test with a sharp prediction and an honest power statement.
5. Weeks 13-15, Mexico. Signed surprises: MXN's beta to negative oil surprises (price falls) should be smaller than to positive ones, and smaller in years with a higher hedged share or strike relative to spot (2001-2017 where disclosed); COP and BRL as unhedged comparators. Deliverable: an asymmetric-beta test mirroring the option payoff, with Mexico's small net-export base acknowledged.
6. Weeks 15-17, tradability. One pre-registered OPEC-day rule: on an adverse supply surprise, long an exporter basket weighted by (exposure minus hedge) and short importers, held 1-5 days; evaluate after bid-ask costs with Sharpe, drawdown, a White (2000) reality check across all rules tried, and a Kelly fraction with a bankroll path. "Priced in the window, not tradable" is the efficient-market result.
7. Weeks 17-20, robustness and write-up. Re-run steps 1-5 with Degasperi's clean supply component, 2-day windows and VIX/S&P controls (Mori-Peersman); average across the two instruments in the spirit of Dr Gifuni's model-uncertainty work. Optional only: exporters' 2-year yields on OPEC days to test for an expected monetary offset.

### A. The Atlantic Switch

**Scores:** originality 8.0, quant appeal 8.7 (second highest of all 17, after G), prize and mark 7.7, feasibility 5.3, Gifuni fit 7.0. Ranked second overall by all three judges.

**The question.** When the EIA's weekly storage number surprises the US gas market at 16:30 Amsterdam time, how much of the Henry Hub reaction reaches Dutch TTF and British NBP prices by that evening's settlement? And is the pass-through switched on only when US LNG export capacity is slack and a cargo is at the margin of cancellation?

**One-sentence hook.** A scheduled US number lands 35 minutes before TTF's 17:05-17:15 settlement window; I use it as an instrument to measure what fraction of a US gas shock crosses the Atlantic, and show the fraction is a switch controlled by whether an LNG cargo is worth loading.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** No published or working paper measures the transmission of the EIA storage surprise to any non-US gas price. The US literature stops at Henry Hub: Ederington, Lin, Linn and Yang (2019, The Energy Journal 40(5), DOI 10.5547/01956574.40.5.lede) on the US reaction and analyst forecast informativeness; Prokopczuk, Wese Simen and Wichmann (2021, The Energy Journal 42(2), DOI 10.5547/01956574.42.2.mpro) on the announcement-day return premium; Gu, Kurov and Stan (2026, Journal of Futures Markets 46(7), DOI 10.1002/fut.70104) on the weaker reaction to holiday-shifted Friday releases, which pre-empts the holiday-shift idea as a design; Halova, Kurov and Kucher (2014, JFM 34(10), DOI 10.1002/fut.21633) on Rigobon-Sack identification of intraday reactions; Chen, Hartley and Lan (2023, JFM 43(4), DOI 10.1002/fut.22402) on storage announcement effects with temperature controls; Chebbi (2019, DOI 10.1504/ijgei.2019.100685). Halova Wolfe and Rosenman (2014, Energy Economics 42, DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2013.12.010) use scheduled EIA announcements as identified shocks for oil-gas transmission inside the US, so the idea of cross-market transmission via a scheduled release exists, but has never been pointed across the Atlantic or conditioned on the physical arbitrage state. Fernández-Pérez, Garel and Indriawan (2020, The Energy Journal 41(5), DOI 10.5547/01956574.41.5.afer) and Ederington et al. together mean "are analyst storage forecasts rational?" is largely answered, so that step is a data-quality check, not a contribution. The integration literature (Farag, Jeddi and Kopp 2025, The World Economy 48(6), DOI 10.1111/twec.13699; Nick and Tischler 2014, EWI Working Paper 14/06) uses cointegration or causality on price levels with no identified shock.

He can also advise on reporting a null honestly. He cannot help with LNG cost chains, capacity data or ICE settlement rules; you self-teach those from free EIA, GIE, OIES and ICE documents.

**Why a quant interviewer will like it, and what it proves.** A desk trades precisely this: release, consensus, surprise, reaction, spillover. The interviewer sees exogenous timing, an IV estimate of pass-through (the surprise instruments the US move, so the European coefficient is a clean fraction of the US-specific shock), a physical no-arbitrage switch (feedgas utilisation and the TTF minus 1.15 times Henry Hub minus shipping minus regasification margin), placebo Thursdays, a pre-registered minimum-detectable-effect calculation, and a lead-lag rule net of costs with a Kelly bankroll. Skills proved: event-study and IV econometrics with HAC and bootstrap, regime regression, forecast evaluation, dataset assembly from six free sources, and exact market-timing knowledge (10:30 ET is 16:30 CET except for about three daylight-saving weeks a year; TTF settles 17:05-17:15 CET).

**Method steps.**
1. ### F. Does the Dealer Lie About the Deck? OPEC's Demand Forecasts as Strategic Signals

**Scores:** originality 8.0, quant appeal 7.7, prize and mark 7.7, feasibility 5.7, Gifuni fit 7.3. Ranked third overall by two judges and fourth by the third. Near-duplicate of idea D below; pitch one of the two (the judges prefer F).

**The question.** Are OPEC's monthly world-oil-demand forecasts biased relative to the EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook (and the IEA where free), in a direction, at horizons and at calendar points that track OPEC's own production stance and meeting cycle rather than its information set? And has the futures market learned to discount OPEC's revisions when the cartel's recent record has been poor?

**One-sentence hook.** Every month the player with the biggest stack tells the table how strong demand will be; I treated 25 years of OPEC's forecasts as cheap talk from an interested party, scored them vintage by vintage against the EIA and against OPEC's own later numbers, and tested whether the market has learned to discount the dealer.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** Forecast-evaluation work on energy agencies covers the EIA only (Auffhammer 2007, Resource and Energy Economics 29(2); Sanders, Manfredo and Boris 2008, Energy Economics 30(3); Garratt, Petrella and Zhang 2023, Energy Economics 121, DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2023.106620) or the IEA's long-run World Energy Outlook (Wachtmeister, Henke and Höök 2018, Applied Energy) and never asks whether a forecaster with a stake in the price shades its numbers. The IEA-OPEC demand gap is discussed only in a Reuters tally 2008-2023 (reproduced at sweetcrudereports.com), a 2008 Deutsche Bank note (via Oil and Gas Journal), a 2024 Baker Institute brief (Finley) and the IEF's monthly comparisons, none of which test bias formally, link it to OPEC's stance or calendar, or measure market learning. The only cheap-talk test on OPEC (Brunetti, Büyükşahin and Robe 2013, The Energy Journal 34(4), DOI 10.5547/01956574.34.4.5) covers "fair price" statements, not demand forecasts. Moghaddam (2019, OPEC Energy Review, DOI 10.1111/opec.12138) tests efficiency and herding in oil price forecasts, not demand. Release-day surprise designs exist for the EIA weekly inventory report (Bu 2014; Ye and Karali 2016; Miao et al. 2018) but have never been applied to agency demand revisions. KAPSARC (2018, DP40, DOI 10.30573/ks--2018-dp40) documents the secondary-sources versus direct-communication production gap used here as an honesty measure. Känzig (2021) supplies OPEC decision dates for stance regimes. Coibion and Gorodnichenko (2015, AER 105(8)), Nordhaus (1987), Ottaviani and Sørensen (2006), Laster, Bennett and Geoum (1999) and Crawford and Sobel (1982) supply methods and theory, never applied to OPEC. No paper on MOMR demand-forecast bias, strategic forecasting by OPEC, or market reactions to MOMR revisions was found.

**Why Dr Gifuni will find it interesting, and what he can advise on.** Forecast evaluation is his core craft: MSPE ratios, DM, Mincer-Zarnowitz, Model Confidence Set, recursive real-time design, all in his public repository and all used here on a new object. His brief topic 3 asks how institutions respond to projections of commodity prices; this asks whether an institution's projections are themselves strategic, which he will see as a natural twist. The text chapter scores the MOMR's demand section with the Loughran-McDonald and Financial Stability dictionaries from his IJF paper and tests whether tone leads numerical revisions, echoing his SARB finding that message components rather than whole documents move markets. His public Oil_Variables.xlsx and TOSI are natural controls (does news sentiment predict OPEC's revisions?). He can advise on every test from published methods; the Kilian demand/supply framing is his home ground.

**Why a quant interviewer will like it, and what it proves.** It mirrors the sell-side analyst-bias research quant funds already use: a sender with a known incentive, a receiver who should discount, and a test of whether the discount is applied and updated. It shows vintage discipline (no look-ahead), a horse race with the right small-sample tests, a surprise built on release days against the rival agency's latest revision, a Bayesian reliability weight that doubles as a forecast combination, horizon and calendar tests that separate strategy from model differences, and a pre-registered "fade the cartel's optimism" signal. Interviewers can probe why revisions predict errors under information rigidity, how to measure error when the outcome itself is revised, and how posterior weights update.

**Method steps.**
1. Build the vintage panel (weeks 1-4). From each MOMR and STEO (and each free IEA issue), record the world-demand forecast for the current and next year, the previous month's figure, the revision, and the MOMR's secondary-source and direct-communication OPEC production figures. Write-up weeks 22-26.

### D. Who Moves First?

**Scores:** originality 8.0, quant appeal 7.7, prize and mark 7.3, feasibility 4.7 (lowest of the strong ideas), Gifuni fit 6.7. Near-duplicate of F (same MOMR and STEO panel). The judges prefer F because it is more feasible, has a sharper incentive story and a survivable week-1 fallback; D has the richer three-agency lead-lag design. Pitch one.

**The question.** Each month the EIA, OPEC and the IEA publish, usually in that order and days apart, forecasts of world oil demand growth for the current and next year. Scored against each agency's own final estimate and against JODI, who is more accurate? Does the later report's revision follow the earlier report's beyond what its own information warrants? Does OPEC's gap to the other two track OPEC's production-policy state and the March 2022 break (when OPEC+ dropped the IEA as a secondary source)? And does Brent react to the later release once the earlier one is already public?

**One-sentence hook.** Three institutions post rival oil-demand forecasts days apart every month and have been further apart in 2024 and 2026 than at any time since 2008; I score who has the edge over twenty years, who follows whom, whether the cartel's number tilts with its policy, and whether Brent has already read their cards.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** No academic study formally evaluates or compares the IEA, OPEC and EIA monthly demand forecasts. The record is a Reuters first-versus-December count (2024, https://www.brecorder.com/news/40295806), a Deutsche Bank note (Lewis 2008, via https://www.ogj.com/general-interest/article/17267672/reliability-of-oil-supply-demand-forecasts-challenged), a Baker Institute note (2024, https://www.bakerinstitute.org/research/whats-happening-oil-market-forecasts) and the IEF's descriptive tables (from about 2020). Garratt, Petrella and Zhang (2023) evaluate the EIA alone to 2019. Fixed-event efficiency tests (Nordhaus 1987, REStat 69(4), DOI 10.2307/1935962; Clements 1997, Journal of Forecasting 16(4); Lahiri and Zhao 2020, Economics Letters 193 / CESifo WP 8350) have been applied to oil price forecasts (Moghaddam, Dehnavi and Wirl 2018, OPEC Energy Review 43(1), DOI 10.1111/opec.12138; Otero et al. 2025, Journal of Commodity Markets 40, with paid Consensus data) but never to the agencies' free demand forecasts. The only release-day event study is a 2006 Taiwanese master's thesis on IEA releases 1990-2005 (Chen 2006, https://nccur.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/34053); the many OPEC event studies (Lin and Tamvakis 2010; Demirer and Kutan 2010; Loutia et al. 2016; Känzig 2021; IMF WP 2022/183) concern meetings, not the monthly report. The March 2022 secondary-source break is documented by S&P Global and Argus (https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news/2317246-opec-mulls-removing-iea-as-secondary-source-correction).

Honest caveats: his text expertise is barely used unless the optional hedging step is run; his third brief topic is about institutions responding to projections, not scoring the institutions that produce them, so say the fit is through methods and credibility rather than claiming a verbatim match. Herding theory and fixed-event tests he has not published on.

Skills proved: panel and time-series econometrics, event-study design, careful treatment of information sets and vintages, and the discipline to report a null market result as a finding.

**Method steps.**
1. Week-1 gate and three-agency fixed-event panel (weeks 1-8). 4. OPEC's tilt (weeks 14-16). ### E. The Government Put: Scoring Forty Years of SPR Promises

**Scores:** originality 8.0, quant appeal 7.3, prize and mark 6.3, feasibility 6.7, Gifuni fit 7.3. Competes with F and J for the policy-credibility slot; shares the "government writes an option" framing with existing idea 3.

**The question.** When Washington announces an SPR release, exchange or repurchase, does the oil market price the barrels announced or the barrels the Department of Energy's forty-year record says it will deliver? And did the quantity-limited $67-72 refill band of October 2022 leave the footprint of a credible floor in the deferred WTI curve, fading as the purchasing budget was spent and as DOE itself bought above the band?

**One-sentence hook.** In October 2022 the US government wrote a put on WTI struck at $67-72 with a notional of about five billion dollars; I built what I believe is the first ledger of what the SPR promised versus what it delivered since 1985, tested whether traders learned to discount the announcer, and asked whether a put the writer could barely fund was ever priced.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** The SPR literature estimates average price effects with structural VARs (Kilian and Zhou 2020, JAE 35(6), DOI 10.1002/jae.2798; Stevens and Zhang 2021, The Energy Journal 42(6), DOI 10.5547/01956574.42.6.rste) or announcement event studies that take announced volumes at face value (Demirer and Kutan 2010, Energy Economics 32(6), DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2010.06.006; Stevens 2014, UC Berkeley working paper, with high-frequency futures; Kelly 2023, Journal of Business Administration Online, on equities, the only study to separate exchanges from sales). Newell and Prest (2017, NBER WP 23974) simulate releases in a VAR of the futures curve and inventories. Razek et al. (2023, Resources Policy 86) and Conte (2022, SUNY repository) are the only work on the 2021-22 releases. Theory: Salant (1983, JPE 91(1), RAND P-6766) on speculative attack against a finite buffer stock; Krugman and Rotemberg (1990/91) and Flood and Garber (1989, NBER WP 2918) on band credibility as a function of reserves; Svensson (1991, IMF Staff Papers 38(3), NBER WP 3394) and Flood, Rose and Mathieson (1991) on target-zone credibility tests, applied only to exchange rates and one inflation target. Hammoudeh and Madan (1995), Tang and Hammoudeh (2002) and Bharati, Crain and Kaminski (2012) apply target-zone methods to OPEC's own 1988-2003 price band, a seller-side zone. US Treasury press release jy0887 (26 July 2022) gives an elasticity-based estimate of the 2022 releases' effect. No paper found has (a) assembled an announce-versus-award-versus-deliver ledger and scored DOE as a forecaster of its own actions, (b) tested whether announcement-day reactions per barrel scale with a real-time credibility posterior, (c) treated the 2022-25 refill band as a Salant buffer-stock floor whose credibility depends on observable remaining reserves (the SPR Petroleum Account), or (d) covered the January 2023 rejected bids, the 2024 above-band purchases, the 2025 fill-to-the-top pledge funded with $171m, or the March 2026 172 mb exchange with its 200 mb return pledge.

The optional text step scores DOE and White House releases with his Loughran-McDonald and Financial Stability dictionaries and re-tests his finding that uncertainty language forecasts poorly, in a setting where hedging language is the signal. It shows a dataset no paper found uses, a clean event study with HAC and bootstrap placebos, a beta-binomial credibility tracker derivable on a whiteboard, a limited-reserves attack model turned into a testable fade-out hypothesis, and two pre-registered Kelly-sized rules whose honest failure is reported as a result. Skills proved: time-series econometrics, event-study design, Bayesian updating, options and term-structure reasoning, out-of-sample discipline.

### G. Pricing the Wednesday: the oil options market's price for one EIA report

**Scores:** originality 6.0, quant appeal 9.0 (highest), prize and mark 7.0, feasibility 8.7 (highest), Gifuni fit 6.0 (lowest of the kept ideas). All three judges named it the cannot-fail reserve; one ranked it third overall.

**The question.** How much implied variance does the crude oil options market assign to a single scheduled EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report? Is that price fair against the extra variance report days actually deliver (a scheduled-news variance risk premium)? And does the premium rise when commercial stocks are low relative to seasonal norms or when text-based oil uncertainty is high?

**One-sentence hook.** The Tuesday-to-Wednesday close is the only step of the week that removes an EIA report from OVX's 30-day window without changing the number of trading days in it, so a free index and a calendar are enough to read off what traders pay for one Wednesday and to test whether they overpay.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** What exists: implied volatility falls after scheduled releases and weekday patterns come from release timing (Ederington and Lee 1996, JFQA 31(4), DOI 10.2307/2331358, for rates and FX, the methodological ancestor); OVX falls on WPSR days (López 2018, Energy Economics 72, DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2018.04.040; Nikkinen and Rothovius 2019, Energy 166, DOI 10.1016/j.energy.2018.10.061; Sharma 2017, University of Georgia MS thesis, https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/14104); OVX has Monday and Friday effects (Qadan and Idilbi-Bayaa 2021, Resources Policy 71, DOI 10.1016/j.resourpol.2020.101980); futures and options prices react to inventory surprises (Miao, Ramchander, Wang and Yang 2018, JFM 38(1), DOI 10.1002/fut.21850); realised volatility is higher on report days (Bu 2014, Energy Economics 46, DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2014.05.015; Ye and Karali 2016, Energy Economics 59, DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2016.08.011); pre-announcement leakage in energy options (Bohmann and Patel 2020, Journal of Derivatives 27(4)); event variance premia exist for FOMC and earnings in equities and rates (Londono and Samadi 2023, FRB IFDP 1376, DOI 10.17016/IFDP.2023.1376; Wright 2020, NBER WP 28306; Alexiou, Goyal, Kostakis and Rompolis 2025, Review of Finance 29(4), DOI 10.1093/rof/rfaf016; Dubinsky, Johannes, Kaeck and Seeger 2019, RFS 32(2), DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhy060); the VIX resolution drop after FOMC (Fernandez-Perez, Frijns and Tourani-Rad 2017; Amengual and Xiu 2018); unconditional energy variance premia are negative (Trolle and Schwartz 2010, Journal of Derivatives 17(3); Morelli 2022, Energy Journal 43(SI1)); OPEC meetings and oil implied volatility (Horan, Peterson and Mahar 2004, Energy Journal 25(3)); the gas announcement-day return premium (Prokopczuk, Wese Simen and Wichmann 2021). What was not found in the literature: (a) the implied variance of one report in variance units, identified by the difference between the Tuesday-to-Wednesday and Monday-to-Tuesday transitions with holiday-week placebos; (b) that implied event variance set against the realised extra variance of report days to form a scheduled-news variance risk premium for a commodity; (c) conditioning of that premium on inventories relative to seasonal norms and on text-based oil uncertainty, including an out-of-sample test of Dr Gifuni's public uncertainty indices on a second-moment target; (d) a Kelly-sized synthetic variance position with a pre-registered rule. The drop itself is a replication and must be presented as such.

**Why Dr Gifuni will find it interesting, and what he can advise on.** It sits in his stated interest in the uncertainty effects of oil price spikes and in oil price forecasting and evaluation. His IJF paper used OVX as an external benchmark and concluded text-based uncertainty indices have "inherent weaknesses" for the oil price level; step 6 re-tests those same public indices (UncVar, OPU in his repository) on the forecast error of implied event variance, a second-moment target, so he gets a direct, respectful extension of his own negative result. He presented at the EIA Financial Markets Workshop in 2025, so the EIA report as an object of study is natural to him. His evaluation toolkit (Mincer-Zarnowitz-type regressions, DM, scoring rules, crisis sub-samples) is what steps 4-6 use. He has not worked on options or variance premia, so the derivatives logic comes from Trolle-Schwartz, Carr-Wu, Wright and Londono-Samadi; his help is on uncertainty measures, forecast evaluation and framing.

**Why a quant interviewer will like it, and what it proves.** It is a question about whether a known, scheduled risk is priced correctly, which is how event-volatility desks think. The interviewer can probe: additivity of event and diffusive variance in a 30-day window; what the VIX-style interpolation between two expiries does to the report count; why Tuesday-to-Wednesday versus Monday-to-Tuesday is the clean comparison; separating resolution from news with the Wednesday intercept; the theory-of-storage prediction for low inventories; HAC inference with persistent regressors; the limits of a synthetic variance swap with no option prices; Kelly under estimation error. Skills shown: event studies, GARCH and HAR, HAC inference, placebo design, out-of-sample DM tests, bet sizing, and a self-built release calendar no one else appears to have. It connects to your delta-hedging simulator on GitHub.

**Method steps.**
1. Build the calendar and OVX anatomy (weeks 1-3): for every trading day from 10 May 2007, record WPSR release day (Wednesday, or the holiday Thursday), API release (Tuesday 16:30 ET), FOMC decision days, OPEC Conference days, and the trading-day and calendar-day counts in the forward 30-day window. Document OVX's weekday pattern and note that the VIX-style interpolation makes the window's report count fractional. A reusable dataset.
2. Estimate the resolution drop by difference-in-transitions (weeks 3-5): regress the daily change in OVX squared on transition dummies; one report's implied variance is the Tuesday-to-Wednesday change minus the Monday-to-Tuesday change (both lose one calendar day and keep 22 trading days; only the first loses a report), with Newey-West errors, FOMC Wednesdays and OPEC days dummied, March-June 2020 excluded. Placebo: in holiday weeks the drop must move to Wednesday-to-Thursday. Convert to annualised variance points and OVX points.
3. Separate "resolution" from "news" (weeks 5-6): on report days regress the change in OVX squared on the absolute inventory surprise (seasonal ARIMA forecast error of the weekly stock change, checked against Investing.com consensus where available) and on the report-day squared return; the intercept is the implied variance of a report net of news, the slope is volatility feedback.
4. Measure the realised counterpart (weeks 6-8): with front-month WTI returns (EIA contract 1 with roll days removed, FRED spot after April 2024 with the splice tested), estimate the excess variance of report days using Tuesday-close-to-Wednesday-close returns in a GARCH(1,1) and a HAR model with a report-day dummy, and check against the simple ratio of mean squared returns; repeat for Henry Hub Thursdays (realised only).
5. Form the scheduled-news variance risk premium (weeks 8-9): implied event variance minus realised event variance, set against the ordinary-day premium; test whether the ratio exceeds one (Londono and Samadi) with block-bootstrap and HAC inference; run the VIX-FOMC analogue as validation.
6. Mechanisms and the text test (weeks 9-11): regress the event premium and realised event variance on commercial stocks relative to their five-year seasonal average, the OVX level, daily GPR and EPU, and Dr Gifuni's monthly text uncertainty indices and OPU; then a recursive out-of-sample test of whether the text uncertainty indices predict (realised minus implied) event variance, with DM tests against a no-change benchmark using the DM code in his repository.
7. Economic value (weeks 11-13): a synthetic 30-day variance position (strike OVX squared, payoff realised variance) entered at Tuesday close only when the estimated event premium is positive, sized by fractional Kelly, with a bid-ask and jump-approximation haircut and a rule pre-registered on 2007-2016 and tested on 2017-2026. State plainly that without option prices this is an upper bound on the edge.

**MATLAB notes.** Core MATLAB plus Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox (fitlm, regress) and Econometrics Toolbox (garch, egarch, estimate, hac, arima). Calendar logic with datetime/weekday and hand-typed federal holiday lists; HAR regressions are OLS; DM, block bootstrap and fractional Kelly are short hand-written functions. No Financial, Optimization or Text Analytics Toolbox. R fallback: fredr/quantmod, rugarch, sandwich and lmtest, forecast::dm.test.

Reconstruct WPSR release dates 2007-2026 from archived EIA schedule snapshots (expect about 980 releases, roughly 150-190 holiday Thursdays) and confirm each against the week-ending-plus-five-days rule. Compute the mean log change in OVX for each weekday transition. GO if (a) Tuesday-to-Wednesday minus Monday-to-Tuesday is negative with |t| above 2 over the full sample, (b) in holiday weeks the drop moves to Wednesday-to-Thursday, and (c) the magnitude is plausible (a report day with about twice a normal day's variance implies roughly 4-5% of window variance, a 2-2.5% fall in OVX, about 0.6-1.0 points at OVX = 35). ### K. The Forecaster's Tell: EIA Prose versus the Options Band

**Scores:** originality 7.0, quant appeal 6.7, prize and mark 7.0, feasibility 7.0, Gifuni fit 9.0. The trimmed, sharper version of idea C below; pitch one of the two (the judges prefer K). The best compromise between supervisor fit and quant content.

**The question.** When the EIA hedges more in the crude-oil section of its monthly Short-Term Energy Outlook, is its own WTI point forecast further off, does the realised price break the options-implied 95% band printed in the same issue more often, and is realised WTI variance higher relative to OVX over the following month, beyond what band width, OVX and the EIA's own numeric deviation from the NYMEX futures price it reports already say? ### L. Does the Sentence Change the Beta? Central-Bank Energy Talk and Oil's Grip on Breakeven Inflation

**Scores:** originality 6.3, quant appeal 5.7 (lowest of the kept ideas), prize and mark 6.7, feasibility 5.0, Gifuni fit 9.7 (highest of all 17). The three model passes disagreed most on this one: one ranked it fourth overall, the others thirteenth and fourteenth.

**The question.** When the Bank of England and the Federal Reserve (ECB and Norges Bank as extensions) devote more of a policy statement to energy and write that passage with firmer, clearer look-through language, does oil's daily pass-through into 5- and 10-year breakeven inflation fall in the inter-meeting period that follows, after controlling for the policy surprise, oil volatility, the inflation level and TIPS liquidity? **One-sentence hook.** Central banks say they look through energy shocks; I measure whether traders believe the sentence or the oil price.

### B. Numbers at 10:30, Words at 1:00

**Scores:** originality 6.7, quant appeal 6.3, prize and mark 6.0, feasibility 5.0, Gifuni fit 9.0.

**The question.** The EIA releases hard numbers at 10:30 ET (WPSR on Wednesday; storage report on Thursday) and its own prose hours later. At 1:00 pm Wednesday two agency texts arrive together: the WPSR's own "Summary" prose, which explains that week's numbers, and This Week in Petroleum (January 2002 to October 2025), which is often a topical essay. The Natural Gas Weekly Update follows on Thursday afternoon (from January 2002; Thursday release from the week of 8 April 2002, Monday before that). Net of the full numeric release, does the tone, uncertainty language and clarity of the agency's own words carry information for oil and gas returns and volatility, and is it priced at once or with a drift?

**One-sentence hook.** Engelberg showed equities are slow to price the words in earnings releases; I built what I believe is the first corpus of the EIA's own weekly explanations, which arrive hours after its numbers, and tested whether anyone reads them.

**Why it is original, and the closest existing work.** No study uses the EIA's weekly narratives as a text corpus. Jeong and Ahn (2025, "Energy organization sentiment and oil return forecast", Energy Economics 141, 108105, DOI 10.1016/j.eneco.2024.108105) score monthly IEA and OPEC reports with a large language model and show agency-report sentiment predicts oil returns (OPEC dominates), so the broad claim is taken. Brunetti, Joëts and Mignon (2024, FEDS 2024-003, DOI 10.17016/FEDS.2024.003) use topic models on OPEC statements. Engelberg (2008, SSRN 1107998, "Costly Information Processing: Evidence from Earnings Announcements", unpublished working paper) is the hard-versus-soft design for earnings releases. Armesto, Hernández-Murillo and Owyang (2009, JMCB 41(1), DOI 10.1111/j.1538-4616.2008.00186.x) show Beige Book tone predicts macro aggregates. Loughran, McDonald and Pragidis (2019, "Dissemination of Oil News into Prices", IRFA 63, 105-118, DOI 10.1016/j.irfa.2019.03.008) use oil news tone for high-frequency returns. Ederington et al. (2019) and Halova, Kurov and Kucher (2014) study the numbers only. Cao and Robe (2022, JFM 42(2), 250-275, online 2021, DOI 10.1002/fut.22283) study uncertainty around USDA reports, not report text. Sobrinho and Malaquias (2025, Commodities 4(4):21, DOI 10.3390/commodities4040021) score official harvest reports' tone. Gifuni (2026) and Gifuni and Ravazzolo (2025) supply methods and the second-moment gap. What is new: the weekly EIA corpus (WPSR summary as the primary 1 pm oil text, TWIP secondary, NGWU for gas), orthogonalisation of words against numbers released the same day, the number-time versus prose-time gap, and the second-moment test.

**Why Dr Gifuni will find it interesting, and what he can advise on.** Text-as-data for energy prices is his core craft and his brief asks for unstructured data to improve oil price forecasts. The dictionaries he used are public, so you replicate his index construction on a new corpus; his repository states no licence, so ask before redistributing anything derived from them. Three hooks: section-level clarity (his SARB paper), the second-moment test of uncertainty language (the gap his IJF paper leaves), and comparing EIA tone with his public TOSI. He can advise on dictionary choice, PCA aggregation, recursive standardisation, DM, CRPS and AUROC evaluation and the pitfalls of text indices. He will find a new official-source energy corpus interesting.

**Why a quant interviewer will like it, and what it proves.** Framed as hard versus soft information with an institutional timing gap, speed of absorption, drift and a rule net of costs, it is a market-mechanism paper. Skills proved: an NLP pipeline built from scratch in MATLAB with no black box, orthogonalisation of text against the full numeric release to kill mechanical correlation, event-day and drift regressions with HAC and permutation inference, free Dukascopy 1-minute data separating the 10:30 and 13:00 ET reactions, multiple-testing discipline, and an out-of-sample rule with Kelly sizing.

Coverage: weekly numbers from 1982/1990, 10:30 ET Wednesday; the full report with its "Summary" prose is posted at 1:00 pm ET; an archive of past summary texts is unconfirmed and is the first week-1 item.
- **EIA This Week in Petroleum archive** (secondary, topical oil corpus). https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/weekly/archive/. Free. Coverage: week ending 11 January 2002 to 29 October 2025 (discontinued; archive retained), Wednesday 1:00 pm ET, about 1,240 issues. From search snippets ("We no longer publish TWIP"); archive not fetched.

Coverage: weekly from January 2002 to at least 15 January 2026, about 1,200 issues; Thursday release by 5:00 pm ET from the week of 8 April 2002 (Monday before); live series at risk from EIA's 2026 report cuts. Issue URLs for several years found via search; pages not fetched.

**Method steps.**

2. 3. Speed: same-day versus 1-5 day drift of residual tone (Engelberg design). With Dukascopy 1-minute data, compare WTI and Brent returns in 10:30-10:45 and 13:00-13:30 ET on Wednesdays, attributing the 1 pm window to the WPSR summary (the text that explains the numbers) and treating TWIP as the secondary corpus; report the 1 pm result separately for weeks when TWIP is a topical essay. For gas, NGWU arrives after the 14:30 NYMEX settlement, so test Friday's return and state the asymmetry.
4. Second moment and clarity: uncertainty language versus OVX and realised volatility; section-level readability versus reaction. Optional: compare the EIA's words with OPEC's (MOMR tone, following Jeong and Ahn) in a matched monthly sample.
5. Tradability and discipline: a pre-registered rule on residual tone net of fees and spread, Sharpe and Kelly bankroll, permutation test, Romano-Wolf correction over the short pre-registered list.

**Cons.**

The WPSR summary text archive is unconfirmed; without it the 1 pm oil corpus is TWIP alone, which is often topical. Consensus history must be assembled from unverified calendar exports. The Text Analytics Toolbox may not be licensed. Dukascopy quotes are broker CFDs, not exchange data.
- *Where you could get stuck.* TWIP essays are often topical features rather than commentary on the week's numbers, which weakens the "words explaining the number" story for oil; the WPSR summary and NGWU are the cleaner corpora. Soft-information effects are tiny; with about 1,200 weekly events per market, power to detect a 5-10 basis-point drift is modest and the dictionary-and-horizon search temptation is strong. Gas prose arrives after the NYMEX settlement, so the oil-versus-gas speed comparison is not symmetric.
- *How examiners might attack it.* "Jeong and Ahn already show agency-report sentiment predicts oil returns." "Tone is partly mechanical ('inventories rose' reads negative); your residual is noise." Residual-tone orthogonalisation and the weather-section placebo must be impeccable. "Two texts arrive at 1 pm; which does the reaction belong to?" Answer: the WPSR summary is primary because it explains the numbers, and TWIP weeks are reported separately. ### Plain-English glossary for the methods named above

- **PIT histogram.** Probability integral transform. For each outcome, the probability the forecast density placed on values at or below what actually happened. If the density is right these numbers are uniform between 0 and 1 and the histogram is flat.
- **Berkowitz test.** Converts PITs to normal scores and tests jointly that they have mean zero, variance one and no autocorrelation: a likelihood-ratio test of calibration.
- **Knüppel test.** A calibration test on moments of the PITs that stays valid when forecasts overlap at horizons beyond one step.
- **CRPS.** Continuous ranked probability score. The average squared distance between the forecast's cumulative distribution and the step function at the outcome. Lower is better; for a point forecast it equals the absolute error.
- **Log score.** The log of the density the forecast placed on the outcome. Higher is better. The average difference in log score between two forecasts is the Kelly growth rate (below).
- **Mincer-Zarnowitz.** Regress the outcome on the forecast. An unbiased, efficient forecast gives intercept 0 and slope 1; test both.
- **HAC (Newey-West, Hodrick).** Standard errors that stay valid when regression errors are autocorrelated and heteroskedastic, as with overlapping multi-month forecast errors. Hodrick (1992) is a version that behaves better in small samples for overlapping horizons.
- **Diebold-Mariano with the HLN correction.** Tests whether two forecasts' average loss differs. Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold (1997) correct its small-sample size at longer horizons.
- **Clark-West.** A version of the DM test for nested models that removes the bias against the larger model caused by estimating extra parameters.
- **Forecast encompassing.** Regress the outcome on two forecasts together. If one gets a weight of zero, the other "encompasses" it: it already contains all of its information.
- **White reality check.** A bootstrap test of whether the best of many rules tried beats the benchmark once you account for having searched over all of them.
- **Romano-Wolf.** A step-down procedure that controls the chance of any false rejection across a family of tests, with more power than Bonferroni because it uses the correlation between the tests.
- **Driscoll-Kraay.** Panel standard errors robust to correlation across countries in the same period and over time.
- **Block bootstrap.** Resampling the data in blocks of consecutive observations so that autocorrelation is preserved in the resamples.
- **Permutation test.** Reshuffle the dates or labels, recompute the statistic many times, and read the p-value off that null distribution.
- **Kelly growth.** The long-run growth rate of a bettor's bankroll when each bet is sized to maximise expected log wealth (Kelly 1956). If you hold density p and are offered the benchmark density q as fair odds, the expected log growth equals the average of log p minus log q at the outcomes, which is the log-score gap. It is an identity, not a tradeable profit.
- **Känzig surprise.** The change in oil futures prices in a narrow window around OPEC announcements (Känzig 2021), used as a shock series or instrument for oil supply news.
- **Difference-in-differences.** Compare the change in an outcome for a treated unit before and after a regime change with the change for an untreated unit over the same dates.
- **LDA.** Latent Dirichlet allocation, a statistical topic model that represents each document as a mixture of word-clusters ("topics") learned from the corpus.
